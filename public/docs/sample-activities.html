Sample Activities

These exercises connect a listening question to a mixer action and a short account of what students heard. Use one during class or assign it as a brief homework task.

Prepare a shared recording before the activity so students can spend their time listening. Each exercise below asks for a note. Choose Class: listen and add notes and press Save sharing when students will annotate your split. The chat activities need no more than two or three Listening Guy turns per signed-in student.

1. One Stem at a Time

Use a four-stem split to help students identify instruments by ear and describe each part's role. Leave the recording without introductory notes so students can make their own first observations. The exercise needs no special teaching instruction.

Ask students to play the first minute with every stem, then solo Vocals and replay that minute. They should write one sentence about the voice's pitch, rhythm, or phrasing. Repeat the comparison with Drums, Bass, and Other, then hear bass and drums together and describe how the parts fit. Each student pins a note at a moment that surprised them.

If students describe only the lyrics, ask what the voice does with a phrase. Have them name the instruments they think they hear in Other, then check those guesses against the full mix. For a variation, use six stems to add guitar and piano or ask students to predict the instruments in an unfamiliar genre before they solo a part.

2. Mute the Melody

Choose a recording with a clear lead vocal so students can compare melody and accompaniment. The texture instruction on the Instructor Console page asks Listening Guy to use monophony and polyphony. Students can test whether those terms describe the parts they hear.

Ask students to mute Vocals during the chorus and try to hum the melody. They should identify any remaining stem that carries it. Next, they hear the voice alone and decide what helps them recognize the song. They can then ask Listening Guy whether the texture contains one melodic line or independent lines, and which stems support its answer.

Students solo the named stems and pin a note where the model's description holds or fails. If they equate several instruments with polyphony, ask whether those instruments move together or carry independent lines. The question keeps the description tied to what each part does.

3. Find the Form

Assign one split to each group in a folder that allows class notes. The task asks students to mark section boundaries and explain the sound that led them to each boundary.

Students first listen through at full speed. They then pin notes for the sections they hear, such as an intro, verse, chorus, bridge, solo, or outro. For two boundaries, each group names the stem that signals the change. A new drum pattern, a pause in the bass, or an entrance by backing vocals can give the group a passage to compare.

Have students read the other groups' notes and add a separate note where they disagree. During discussion, use [ and ] to move through the annotations. If a boundary remains hard to hear, try the drum stem or another part that changes at that point.

4. Ask, Then Verify

Use any split to practice checking an AI claim against audible evidence. Reserve two Listening Guy turns per student so each can ask a follow-up after listening.

Students read the opening guide and select one claim about an instrument or section. They solo the relevant stem, find the passage, and use L to loop it. Ask for a three-sentence note. The first sentence states the claim, the second explains the mixer action, and the third describes whether the sound supports the claim.

Students then send a follow-up that includes their evidence and examine whether Listening Guy revises its answer. Ask them to notice when the model acknowledges uncertainty and when it treats a style convention as a fact about the recording. Its guide cannot supply direct evidence from audio that it cannot hear.

5. Slow Rhythm Lab

Choose a recording with a distinctive groove, such as a clave, shuffle, or second-line pattern. Begin with the drum stem at 0.5× so students can count a pattern before they add the bass.

Students loop four bars and count aloud. They write the beats and “ands” as 1 & 2 & … and mark the accents. After they add Bass, they describe whether its attacks land with the kick drum or between those beats. They then return to 1× and check whether they can still hear the pattern they wrote.

A recording with little percussion may leave the drum stem nearly empty. Try Other or Guitar if either contains the rhythmic part students need. Ask students to explain how the chosen stem helped them hear the pattern.

Adapting the Activities

For an asynchronous course, use classmates' notes on a shared split as the starting point for discussion. In a large lecture, lead One Stem at a Time or Slow Rhythm Lab as a live session, then assign Find the Form as homework.

Settings shows students' daily split and chat allowances. A recording you have already split costs students no run to open, so a prepared folder leaves their allowance available for their own experiments.
